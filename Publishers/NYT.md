## Who Is The New York Times

**The New York Times** is the oldest and most influential quality newspaper in the United States. Founded in 1851. Public company (NYSE: NYT). Headquartered in New York City. Annual revenue in 2025 approximately **$2.7 billion** — the first year in company history where total digital revenue exceeded **$2 billion**. Around 1,700 journalists across 160 countries.

The group includes:
- **NYT News** — the core news product
- **The Athletic** — sports (acquired in 2022 for $550M)
- **NYT Cooking** — recipes and culinary content
- **NYT Games** — Wordle, crosswords, puzzles
- **Wirecutter** — product recommendations on an affiliate model
- **NYT Audio** — podcasts and audio articles

This is no longer a newspaper. It is a **media ecosystem** built around a single subscription.

## Monetisation — The Full Picture

### Revenue Structure 2025

| Source | Share | YoY Growth |
|---|---|---|
| **Digital subscriptions** | ~52% | +14% |
| **Print subscriptions** | ~18% | −3% |
| **Digital advertising** | ~15% | +20% |
| **Print advertising** | ~6% | −7% |
| **Affiliate + licensing + other** | ~9% | +8% |

**Subscriptions = ~70% of total revenue.** NYT crossed the inflection point from advertising-led to subscription-led long ago. This fundamentally separates them from most other publishers.

### How the Subscription Model Works

**Bundle strategy** — one subscription, five products. A subscriber pays and gets access to news, sports, cooking, games, and product recommendations simultaneously. Around 50% of subscribers already engage with two or more products. This is critical: a multi-product subscriber almost never cancels, because cancelling means losing everything at once.

- ARPU (average revenue per subscriber) — **$10.50/month** after promotional users transition to full pricing
- Churn for long-term subscribers — **below 2%**
- Target — **15 million subscribers by 2027**

### How Advertising Works

NYT is one of the very few publishers that deliberately **eliminated open programmatic auction** from their mobile app. The rationale: cheap programmatic advertising degrades the user experience — and user experience directly drives subscriptions, which matter far more than incremental ad revenue.

**Three advertising layers:**

**1. Premium direct-sold** — direct deals with major advertisers such as Apple, Goldman Sachs, and Louis Vuitton. The most profitable and most controlled channel.

**2. Closed programmatic premium** — programmatic only through direct partnerships with a limited number of DSPs and SSPs. No open auction. NYT knows every advertiser buying their inventory.

**3. First-party data targeting** — 150+ million registered users give NYT a massive first-party data asset. More than 240 audience segments — by interest, emotion, and behaviour. In a cookieless world, this is their primary advertising weapon.

**Key figure:** users who engage with four or more NYT products weekly are **four times more likely** to make purchases from premium advertisers compared to the general internet audience. That is what NYT sells advertisers — not reach, but depth of engagement.

Digital advertising growth in **Q4 2025: +25% YoY.** This is acceleration, not stagnation.

### AI Content Licensing as a Third Revenue Stream

The Amazon deal (May 2025) opens a new revenue category: **licensing editorial content to AI companies**. Amazon gains access to NYT, NYT Cooking, and The Athletic for model training and integration into Alexa. According to the Wall Street Journal, the deal is worth approximately $20–25M per year. Simultaneously, NYT is suing OpenAI and Perplexity for doing the same thing without paying.

### Wirecutter — A Separate Business Logic

Wirecutter earns through affiliate commissions: a reader clicks a product recommendation link, makes a purchase, and NYT receives a percentage. This is precisely why the OpenAI lawsuit includes specific Wirecutter claims: ChatGPT was reproducing Wirecutter recommendations without linking back to the site, directly cutting off this revenue stream. In one case, ChatGPT attributed a product endorsement to Wirecutter for an item Wirecutter had never actually reviewed — a direct reputational risk.

## Video Player

Historically, NYT has used **Brightcove** — one of the leading enterprise video platforms for media companies. Brightcove is a paid, enterprise-grade solution used by major broadcasters and media brands globally. It gives publishers full control over monetisation, analytics, and advertising — unlike YouTube, which takes a revenue share and removes editorial control over the viewing environment.

For a company of NYT's scale and revenue, relying entirely on a third-party player is becoming less viable. NYT is increasingly building proprietary video infrastructure tailored to their subscription ecosystem — particularly as the CEO named video journalism expansion the top priority for 2026.

NYT also maintains a large **YouTube channel** with millions of subscribers. YouTube pays a revenue share from advertising, but it is a secondary income stream — YouTube takes its cut and NYT have no control over user experience or ad targeting on that platform.

## Paywall — How It Actually Works

NYT operates a **smart, AI-powered dynamic paywall** rather than a simple hard block. It is not "pay or leave" — it is a carefully engineered conversion funnel.

**The system is called Dynamic Meter** — a causal machine learning model that personalises the free article limit for each individual reader. Here is how the funnel works:

**Step 1 — Unregistered visitor:** reads a few articles freely, then hits a **registration wall** — not yet a paywall, simply a prompt to create a free account.

**Step 2 — Registered user:** gets access to more free content. Their reading history, linked to their account ID, feeds the Dynamic Meter model.

**Step 3 — Paywall:** once a registered user reaches their personalised limit, they see the subscription offer.

The key insight: the free article limit is **different for every reader**. Someone who reads frequently and shows strong engagement signals gets more free articles — the model prioritises building a reading habit first. A casual or one-time visitor sees the paywall faster. The Dynamic Meter simultaneously optimises two competing metrics: reader engagement and subscription conversions.

**Recent move — Mini Crossword behind paywall (August 2025):** NYT placed the previously free Mini Crossword puzzle behind a paywall. This caused public backlash among long-term users. But the logic is consistent with their overall strategy: every product interaction should funnel toward subscription. Wordle, Connections, Strands, and Sudoku remain free for now — but the company explicitly did not rule out future changes.

## Video Monetisation — Three Layers

**1. In-player advertising via Brightcove** — pre-roll and mid-roll ads sold directly through premium direct deals, not open programmatic auction. NYT controls who advertises around their content. CPMs are significantly higher than open market rates precisely because of the premium, authenticated audience.

**2. YouTube Partner Program** — NYT's YouTube channel generates advertising revenue through YouTube's standard revenue share. This is a distribution and awareness channel as much as a revenue channel. NYT does not rely on YouTube as a primary monetisation vehicle — the platform's revenue share and lack of editorial control over the ad environment make it unsuitable as a core income source.

**3. Video as a subscription retention tool** — premium video journalism is locked behind the paywall, creating an additional reason to subscribe and stay subscribed. This is the most strategically important video monetisation layer. As the CEO noted in the Q4 2025 earnings call, video is being positioned as a daily habit driver — content that keeps subscribers engaged between breaking news cycles. The more time subscribers spend on video, the higher their ARPU and the lower their likelihood of cancelling.

## Strengths

✅ **Largest paid subscriber base in quality media** — 12.78 million subscribers by end of 2025. No competitor is remotely close.

✅ **Bundle as churn protection** — psychologically, cancelling one product is easy. Cancelling five at once is very hard. This is why churn is below 2%.

✅ **AI used correctly** — for investigations and data analysis, not for writing articles. Cheatsheet has already produced several major investigative pieces that would have been physically impossible without AI.

✅ **First-party data as an advertising asset** — 150M+ registered users in a cookieless world is an enormous advantage over publishers who lack this foundation.

✅ **Clear and consistent legal position** — NYT was the first major publisher to systematically pursue copyright protection for their content in the AI era. The OpenAI and Perplexity lawsuits are an attempt to set an industry-wide precedent.

✅ **AI policy transparency** — publishing internal guidelines publicly builds trust with both readers and journalists. Rare among major newsrooms.

✅ **Diversified ecosystem** — if the news market declines, Cooking and Games compensate. Products act as mutual insurance against any single vertical weakening.

✅ **Record financial performance** — $551M in free cash flow in 2025, operating margin expanding to 19.5% in Q4 2025. This is growth, not survival.

---

## Weak Points

❌ **Paradox: suing OpenAI while using OpenAI** — NYT officially approved journalist use of OpenAI API models while simultaneously suing for "billions of dollars" in damages for the same models' use of their content. A reputational contradiction noticed across the industry.

❌ **AI adoption is slow** — a team of 8 people for a newsroom of 2,000. Cheatsheet was available only to a small group for over a year before the full rollout. Deliberate caution — but also a real speed constraint.

❌ **Internal journalist scepticism** — parts of the newsroom publicly expressed concerns about AI. Cultural resistance requires ongoing management.

❌ **Structural threat from AI search engines** — Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, and ChatGPT answer user questions without sending traffic to NYT's site. Less traffic means fewer subscription conversions. The lawsuit against Perplexity is correct, but lawsuits do not change user behaviour at scale.

❌ **Print continues to decline** — print advertising −7% YoY, print subscriptions −3% YoY. Digital revenues compensate for now, but the structural decay of the print business has not been halted.

❌ **Litigation costs are growing** — Generative AI Litigation Costs already appear as a line item in quarterly filings: $4.6M in Q3 2024 alone. Cases are multiplying.

❌ **Dependence on anchor products** — a significant portion of subscriber growth came from Wordle and The Athletic. If their appeal weakens, churn could accelerate quickly.

❌ **Paywall backlash risk** — the Mini Crossword paywall decision provoked significant public anger. As NYT moves more free content behind the subscription wall, the risk of alienating habitual users increases.

## The Newsroom: How the Day Works

### Philosophy: AI amplifies journalists, it does not replace them

NYT deliberately chose a narrow but deep use case for AI: **investigative journalism and large dataset analysis**. This is a principled public position, backed by enforceable internal policy.

### Internal AI Tools

**Echo** — the internal editorial assistant:
- Headline and SEO headline variants
- Document summarisation
- Interview question preparation
- Social media copy generation
- Code assistance for product teams

**Cheatsheet** — the investigative journalism tool:
- Semantic search across massive datasets ("vibe search" — finding concepts, not keywords)
- Transcription of thousands of hours of video and audio
- Analysis of document leaks, name lists, unstructured data
- Every project is turned into a reusable "recipe" for future journalists

**Manosphere Report** — a weekly AI-generated digest of right-wing podcast content, automatically flagging emerging narratives for journalists to investigate.

### What Is Prohibited by Internal Policy

- ❌ Drafting or substantially revising articles using AI
- ❌ Publishing AI-generated images or videos without clear labelling
- ❌ Bypassing third-party paywalls using AI
- ❌ Trusting AI output without independent journalistic verification

### The Open Slack Channel

Any of the 2,000 newsroom staff can join an open Slack channel to ask questions or share use cases — from "How do I get access to Gemini?" to bureau chiefs sharing workflows across continents. Zach Seward's team has now spoken personally with 1,700 of the 2,000 newsroom staff.

## Notes from My Own Perspective

**What NYT is doing fundamentally right:**

The most important thing is that they understand **trust is their product**. Every decision — from banning AI-written articles to suing OpenAI — is a bet on trust, even when it costs money in the short term. This is a long-term logic, and it is now showing up in their subscriber numbers.

The **Cheatsheet architecture** — building every AI tool as a reusable template from day one — is the correct way to scale AI inside a large newsroom. Not "deploy AI" but "build one good tool and teach everyone to use it."

**What raises questions:**

AI search engines are not just a legal problem. They represent a structural shift in how people find information. Suing Perplexity is the right move, but lawsuits do not change the habits of hundreds of millions of users. NYT needs a **product-level answer** — a reason for users to come to nytimes.com directly rather than receiving answers through an AI intermediary. That answer has not yet been publicly articulated, and it is the most serious strategic question facing them over the next two years.

## NYT vs The Guardian — Direct Comparison

The Guardian is the most instructive comparison for NYT: both are global English-language quality publications, both are building digital futures, but their models are fundamentally different.

| Parameter | NYT 🇺🇸 | The Guardian 🇬🇧 |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue** | ~$2.7B (2025) | ~£275M (~$350M) |
| **Business model** | Hard paywall + subscription | No paywall — voluntary reader contributions |
| **Subscribers / donors** | 12.78M paid subscribers | 365,000 regular donors + one-off contributions |
| **Digital share of revenue** | >70% digital | >70% digital |
| **Primary growth driver** | Bundle subscription (News + Games + Sport + Cooking) | Reader donations, especially from the US |
| **AI deals** | Amazon (~$20–25M/yr); suing OpenAI and Perplexity | Deal with OpenAI (Feb 2025) — attribution, no training data |
| **Legal position on AI** | Aggressive — suing OpenAI, Microsoft, Perplexity | Cooperative — OpenAI deal, Perplexity warning without lawsuit |
| **AI in the newsroom** | Internal tools (Echo, Cheatsheet), strict guidelines | AI-powered reader features via OpenAI partnership |
| **Newsletters** | Core retention tool, dozens of lists | Active strategy, especially for US audience |
| **International expansion** | Canada, UK | US is primary growth market; US revenue >£50M and growing |
| **Primary threat** | AI search engines kill traffic → fewer subscription conversions | Same AI threats, but no paywall means traffic is less critical |
| **Primary strength** | Bundle = low churn = predictable revenue | No paywall = larger audience = more potential donors |
| **Video player** | Brightcove (enterprise) + own infrastructure in development | JW Player |

**The key difference in one sentence:**
NYT monetises the depth of their audience relationship through a paid barrier. The Guardian monetises audience trust through voluntary contribution without a barrier. Both models work — but NYT is scaling far more aggressively.

**What the comparison reveals about AI strategies:**
NYT sues OpenAI and signs a deal with Amazon — maximising the value of their content through negotiating leverage. The Guardian signs with OpenAI and gains access to technology for new products — maximising audience reach through partnership. These are two legitimate answers to the same question: how does a quality media company monetise its content in the AI era?
