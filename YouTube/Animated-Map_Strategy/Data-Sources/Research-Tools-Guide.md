# Nexis, ProQuest, ExpertAccess, Wayback Machine, Quote Investigator, MarineTraffic, TinEye

---

## Introduction

This document answers one practical question: which verification tools to use, how much they cost, and how to access them with minimum expense for an independent educational YouTube creator.

Tools are divided into two groups. Paid archives (Nexis, ProQuest) — for deep historical verification. Free specialized tools (Wayback Machine, Quote Investigator, MarineTraffic, TinEye) — for specific tasks that cannot be solved otherwise.

---

## Part 1 — Nexis (LexisNexis)

### What it is

The world's largest commercial news and business intelligence archive. Operating since the 1970s. Owned by RELX Group.

This is not a search engine — it is a full-text archive. You enter a query and receive the complete text of a New York Times article from 1983, a CNN transcript from 1997, a Reuters dispatch from the day an event happened. Not the headline and first paragraph — the entire piece.

### What is inside

- More than 36,000 sources, 45+ years of archive depth
- Newspapers: New York Times, Financial Times, Washington Post, The Guardian, Le Monde, El País, German publications (Spiegel, Zeit, Welt, FAZ)
- Wire services: Reuters, AP, AFP — full feeds
- Broadcast transcripts: ABC, CBS, CNN, BBC and other broadcasters
- Business intelligence: corporate reports, market analyses
- Legal documents: court decisions, legislation

### What to use it for in verification

Primary scenario: find a wire service dispatch from a specific date. "What did Reuters write about the first oil crisis in October 1973?" — Nexis shows original materials from that day. This is Tier 2 verification in our system: a wire service report contemporaneous with the event.

Second scenario: verify an exact quote. Find a press conference transcript or interview by date and topic.

Third scenario: trace how a topic was covered over time. Date-filtered search shows the evolution of a narrative.

### Price levels

**Corporate (newsrooms, agencies)**
Price is not published. Industry data suggests several thousand to tens of thousands of euros per year per newsroom. This is how Reuters, NYT, and BBC work. Direct contract, unlimited access from anywhere for all staff.

**Direct individual subscription (Nexis Essential)**
LexisNexis sells individual subscriptions online. Exact prices are not published on the website — you must request a quote. Market data suggests approximately €150–300 per month for full news archive access.

**ExpertAccess — optimal option for independent journalists**
This is a separate story and the most important finding. See Part 3.

**University subscription — free for students**
Major universities typically hold institutional licenses. Students and staff access through the university portal from home.

## Part 2 — ProQuest

### What it is

An American company specializing in digitizing and archiving historical publications. Unlike Nexis which is strong on news from the last 40–45 years, ProQuest goes deeper into history — in some cases back to the mid-nineteenth century and earlier.

### What is inside

- New York Times — from 1851 (from the first issue)
- Washington Post — from 1877
- The Guardian / The Observer — from 1821
- Chicago Tribune — from 1849
- Le Monde, Los Angeles Times, and hundreds of other titles
- Academic journals, dissertations, government documents

### What to use it for

ProQuest is essential for historical primary sources. How did the New York Times cover the discovery of oil in Saudi Arabia in 1938? What did the British press write about the founding of OPEC in 1960? This is ProQuest — original newspaper pages in PDF, preserving the original layout.

### Price levels

ProQuest does not sell individual subscriptions to private individuals. Only institutional licenses for libraries and universities.

**For individual access:**
- Through a university — free if you are a student or staff member
- Individual document purchase through ProQuest — approximately $5–15 per document

### Key difference from Nexis

| | Nexis | ProQuest |
|---|---|---|
| Primary strength | News 1980–present | Historical newspapers 1820–1990 |
| Broadcast transcripts | Yes | No |
| Historical depth | 45+ years | 150+ years |
| Academic works | Limited | Extensive |
| Individual subscription | Yes (via ExpertAccess) | Library access only |

## Part 3 — ExpertAccess: Nexis for Independent Journalists

### What it is

ExpertAccess is an intermediary program founded by journalist Brad Hamilton, editor-in-chief of The Hatch Institute (a nonprofit investigative journalism organization in New York). He negotiated special terms with LexisNexis for freelancers.

URL: expertaccess.org

### Why this matters

Before ExpertAccess, full Nexis access for a freelancer cost several hundred dollars per month — out of reach for most independent creators. Hamilton took on the administrative work of managing group contracts in exchange for discounted rates for his community.

### What is included

- Full Nexis archive — approximately 10,000 publications going back to the 1970s
- Direct access to the LexisNexis searchable database
- Unlimited access to New York Times, The New Yorker, The Guardian, Reuters, AP and thousands of other publications
- Specialized trade journals covering business, finance, politics, health, science
- Home access 24/7 — no need to physically visit a library

### What is NOT included

The program does not include Public Records (Accurint database) and CourtLinks. These are separate LexisNexis products costing hundreds of dollars per month each, requiring formation of a legal entity. For fact-checking in educational YouTube they are not needed.

### Cost

$26 per month on an annual contract — $312 per year (approximately €290).

For comparison: a direct individual Nexis subscription costs approximately 6–10 times more.

### Bonus — personal training

The program includes free one-on-one sessions with Brad Hamilton — a veteran investigative journalist who has personally trained hundreds of LexisNexis users including Pulitzer Prize-winning reporters. He shows how to search, how to construct queries, how to find non-obvious sources.

### Who it is for

ExpertAccess is oriented toward American journalists and authors. There are no technical restrictions on access from other countries — but terms of use should be confirmed at registration. For a European user, this is a workable option for regular home access to Nexis.

### Practically: when $26/month is justified

If you produce 2 or more videos per month on topics requiring verification of historical events (1980–2010) — ExpertAccess pays for itself on the first research session. The alternative: pay €2–5 per individual article or travel to a physical library.

## Part 4 — Wayback Machine

### What it is

A non-commercial internet archive maintained by the Internet Archive organization (archive.org). Since 1996 it has been taking snapshots of web pages and storing them permanently.

URL: web.archive.org

### What it does

You enter any URL — and see how that page looked on any date in its history. A Reuters article that now costs €5 and is hidden behind a paywall — its 2003 snapshot is available free. A press release a company deleted from its website — Wayback Machine saved it.

### How to use for verification

**Accessing paywalled articles:**
1. Find the article URL (usually visible in search results even if the article is paywalled)
2. Go to web.archive.org
3. Paste the URL into the search bar
4. Select the snapshot closest to the publication date
5. Read for free

**Proving that a source said exactly what you claim:**
Before publishing a video, create an archived snapshot of every source: go to web.archive.org/save/[your URL]. You receive a permanent link to the snapshot. If in three years World Bank changes its figures — you have proof that on the publication date the source said exactly what you cited.

### Limitations

Not all pages are archived. Some websites block archiving via robots.txt. But major news publications and government sites are well covered.

### Cost

Completely free. Non-commercial project.

## Part 5 — Quote Investigator

### What it is

A research project by one person — Garson O'Toole (a pseudonym). He professionally tracks the origins of famous quotations. Operating since 2010.

URL: quoteinvestigator.com

### The problem it solves

90% of famous quotations are attributed to the wrong people. This is not an exaggeration — it is a documented fact.

A few examples:

"Be yourself — everyone else is already taken" — attributed to Oscar Wilde everywhere. Quote Investigator found no evidence that Wilde ever said this. An early version appeared in the 1950s without attribution.

"The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results" — universally attributed to Einstein. Quote Investigator found no evidence in his writings. The earliest appearance was in Narcotics Anonymous literature in the 1980s.

"Not everything on the internet is true — Abraham Lincoln, 1864" — this is obviously a joke. But variants of this quote with serious attributions genuinely appear online.

### How to use it

Enter the quote or its key phrase into the search on the site. If the quote is well-known — there is probably an article on its origins. Quote Investigator shows:
- The earliest documented appearance
- Who the quote actually belongs to
- How the misattribution occurred
- Primary sources with dates

### Why this is critical for educational YouTube

A quote on screen with the wrong attribution is exactly the type of error that the most attentive viewers find. It is clearly visible, easily verifiable, and stays in the comments for years. Quote Investigator closes this risk in 2 minutes.

### Cost

Completely free.

## Part 6 — MarineTraffic

### What it is

A global platform for tracking maritime vessels in real time. Uses AIS (Automatic Identification System) data — by law every commercial vessel is required to broadcast its signal with coordinates, speed, heading, and cargo type.

URL: marinetraffic.com

### What it does

Right now you can open the site and see every tanker passing through the Strait of Hormuz, every container ship in the Malacca Strait, every vessel in every port in the world. In real time.

Historical data allows you to see how many vessels passed through a specific point on a specific day. For a specific vessel — its route, port call dates, cargo type and quantity.

### Which topics to use it for

Any story about trade, energy, logistics, geopolitics of straits. "17–18 tankers pass through the Strait of Hormuz each day" — this is not a figure from a secondary article, it is a verifiable fact from AIS data.

For a story about sanctions against a specific country — MarineTraffic shows real changes in shipping. For a story about trade routes — historical vessel tracks.

### Access levels

**Free:** Current positions of all vessels in real time. Basic vessel information. Sufficient for most research tasks.

**Paid (from ~€30/month):** Extended historical analytics, cargo data, detailed routes over long periods. Only needed for deep regular analysis of maritime topics.

**VesselFinder** (vesselfinder.com) as an alternative: similar tool. Sometimes has better coverage of specific regions. Use both for cross-checking.

### Cost

Free for basic use.

## Part 7 — TinEye

### What it is

A reverse image search tool with one unique feature — sorting results by date. You can find the earliest known appearance of a specific image on the internet.

URL: tineye.com

### How it differs from Google Reverse Image Search

Google Images also does reverse search — but does not sort by date. TinEye was specifically built to answer the question "when did this photo first appear online?"

**Yandex Images** (yandex.com/images) as a supplement — often finds results that neither Google nor TinEye finds, especially for Eastern European and Middle Eastern content.

### The problem it solves

News sites, YouTube channels, and social media regularly use old photographs with incorrect dates and descriptions. "Photo of protests in country X in 2023" turns out to be a shot from a different country in 2015. "Historical photo of event Y" was actually taken ten years after the event.

TinEye reveals this in 30 seconds.

### How to use it

1. Go to tineye.com
2. Upload an image or paste its URL
3. In the results, select "Oldest" sort order
4. Check: does the earliest appearance correspond to the claimed date?

If a photo "from 2024" appears in TinEye results from 2018 — it has been misdated. Do not use.

### Additional visual verification tools

**InVID/WeVerify** (Chrome/Firefox extension): breaks video into keyframes and runs a reverse search on each one. Essential for verifying footage of events.
URL: invid-project.eu

**Google Earth Historical Imagery**: in desktop Google Earth — View → Historical Imagery. Shows satellite images of any location on Earth from the 1980s onward. Verify "the factory that was built in 2015" — see what the site looked like before and after.

**Sentinel Hub** (apps.sentinel-hub.com): free access to European Space Agency satellite imagery from 2015 onward.

### Cost

Free — basic search with usage limits. Paid plans from $200/year for professional use. For occasional verification, the free plan is sufficient.

## Recommended Strategy by Level

### Level 0 — Starting (€0)
Free sources cover 80% of verification tasks:
- Reuters.com and AP News — news from the 1990s onward
- World Bank, EIA, Eurostat — all data
- Wayback Machine — archived pages
- Quote Investigator — quote verification
- MarineTraffic — maritime data
- TinEye — image verification

### Level 1 — Journalist ($26/month via ExpertAccess)
When the channel produces 2+ videos per month requiring verification of events from 1980–2010.
Adds: full Nexis from home 24/7.
Justified when the value of one found primary source exceeds the monthly subscription cost.

### Level 2 — Professional (€150–400/month)
Direct subscriptions to LexisNexis, Factiva, Lloyd's List.
Justified only when the channel operates commercially with regular content production.

## How to Cite These Sources in Video Descriptions

Every source used in a video must appear in the description. Format:

**For Nexis-found wire report:**
```
[Author or "Reuters Staff"], "[Headline]," Reuters, [Date]
Retrieved via Nexis archive
```

**For ProQuest historical newspaper:**
```
"[Headline]," [Newspaper name], [Date], p.[page number]
Retrieved via ProQuest Historical Newspapers
```

**For Wayback Machine archived page:**
```
[Organization], "[Page title]," [Original URL]
Archived version: web.archive.org/web/[timestamp]/[url]
Accessed: [your access date]
```

**For MarineTraffic vessel data:**
```
MarineTraffic AIS Data, vessel "[name]," [date range]
marinetraffic.com
```
